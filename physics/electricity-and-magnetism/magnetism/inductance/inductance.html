Inductors resist changes in current by inducing emf.

Define inductance, distinguish self and mutual inductance, and interpret energy stored in magnetic fields.

This topic

Inductance

Inductance connects current to magnetic flux, and it explains why circuits with coils respond gradually rather than instantly.

Self
Self-inductance
A changing current in a circuit changes its own magnetic flux, inducing an emf that opposes the change. The constant linking flux to current is the self-inductance.
  • Flux is proportional to current (ideal model)
  • Induced emf opposes current change
  • Inductors act like “inertia” for current
  • Units: henry (H)

Mutual
Mutual inductance (intro)
A changing current in one circuit can change the magnetic flux through a nearby circuit, inducing an emf there. Mutual inductance measures the coupling strength.
  • Two circuits linked by shared magnetic flux
  • Coupling depends on geometry and distance
  • Basis of transformers (conceptual)
  • Direction still follows Lenz’s law

Energy
Energy stored in magnetic fields
When current builds up in an inductor, energy is stored in the magnetic field. This stored energy can be returned later as the field collapses.
  • Energy is tied to field creation
  • Explains why current changes gradually
  • Where the energy goes during decay
  • Connects to transients in RL circuits

Modeling
Physical intuition for inductors
Inductors do not “block” current. They oppose rapid changes. In steady DC, an ideal inductor behaves like a wire; during change, it develops a voltage across it.
  • Opposes dI/dt, not I
  • Steady-state vs transient behavior
  • Voltage appears during current changes
  • Helps interpret RL circuit graphs
